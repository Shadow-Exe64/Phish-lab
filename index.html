The human firewall playbook

Filters miss things. This is the short, practical guide to what attackers do and what to do about it.

Pause · Verify · Report

  1. 1 PauseSpot the trigger (urgency, fear, authority, curiosity, greed) and stop interacting. Give it five minutes.
  2. 2 VerifyConfirm through a second, known channel. If it came by email, phone a number from the company directory, never one in the message.
  3. 3 ReportUse the report button or forward to the security team. Do not just delete: reporting lets them remove it from every inbox.

Triage checklist for non-experts

Work top to bottom. Every message ends with one action. Your ticks are saved in this browser.

Eleven red flags

1 Sender-domain mismatch

The display name says one thing, the routing domain another.

2 Fake forwarded chains

“FW:” threads with pasted headers from conversations you were never in.

3 Browser-in-the-browser

A fake sign-in pop-up that cannot be dragged outside the browser window.

4 Dangerous attachments

.iso, .js, .scr, HTML files or double extensions posing as documents.

5 Urgent bypass requests

Demands for secrecy or skipping normal procedure.

6 Sensitive information

Unexpected asks for MFA codes, passwords or new payment details.

7 Activity alerts

Alarming sign-in warnings that point straight to a login page.

8 MFA fatigue

Repeated unprompted push prompts meant to wear you down until you approve.

9 Callback scams (TOAD)

No link, just a phone number to “fix” a fake charge.

10 QR code prompts

Unsolicited codes that move you to a phone, bypassing desktop filters.

11 Deepfake meetings

Cloned voice or video of an executive demanding mid-call payment changes.

Cognitive triggers

Authority
Impersonating the C-suite, IT or law enforcement to demand compliance.
Urgency
“Account locked in 30 minutes” triggers fight-or-flight and cuts careful thinking.
Curiosity
“See what your colleague said about you.”
Fear / greed
Threats of legal action, or prizes you never entered for.

Who is being targeted?

Mass phishing
Generic lures pretending to be big brands. High volume, low personalisation.
Spear phishing
Uses public information (project names, colleagues) to be believable.
Whaling
Targets executives to authorise large transfers (business email compromise).

Beyond the inbox

Smishing
Malicious texts posing as parcels or bank alerts.
Vishing
Calls with a spoofed caller ID pretending to be IT or a government body.
Quishing
QR codes on posters or PDFs that push you to a phone.
Search-engine phishing
Poisoned results that put a fake login page above the real one.

Look-alike domains

Typosquatting
amaz0n.com: a swapped or missing character.
Homoglyphs
pаypal.com with a Cyrillic “а”: identical to the eye.
Combosquatting
yourcompany-secure-login.com: real name plus security words.
Sub-domain trap
www.decodelabs.tech.login-update.com: the true owner is login-update.com. Read right to left.
Dangling DNS
A deleted cloud resource whose DNS record remains lets an attacker take over a trusted sub-domain.

Running phishing simulations responsibly

Do

  • Mimic real brands and use believable emotional triggers.
  • Include subtle, realistic anomalies such as a slightly misspelled domain.
  • Teach: give instant feedback and micro-learning after a click.

Don't

  • Scare people with family emergencies or termination threats.
  • Use lies that are easily verified, like impersonating the person at the next desk.
  • Chase a 0% click rate with comically bad spelling. Mirror real attackers.
